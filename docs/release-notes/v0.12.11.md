**The answer was right. The quotation supporting it was invented.**

v0.12.10 answered "who approves a contract of exactly EUR 25,000?" with *Head
of Department* — correct — and backed it with what reads as a row of the policy
table:

> "Contract value (annual): Above EUR 25,000 | Approver: Chief Financial
> Officer | Additional requirement: Three competitive quotes"

There is no such row. "Above EUR 25,000" appears in the document as prose about
competitive quotes and is never paired with an approver; the answer welded it to
a different row. The grounding gate scores word and number overlap, and every
word in that sentence is real somewhere in the document, so it saw nothing
wrong. The same blindness passed an invented fourth act of a three-act play two
releases earlier.

**Answers are now checked for whether their quotations are quotations.** A span
in quotation marks long enough to be a claim about what a document says must
actually appear there.

**The obvious version of this check is dangerous, so it was measured first.**
Over every answer this project has kept from its own evaluation runs — 674 of
them, each paired with the documents it cited:

| | |
|---|---:|
| answers containing a quotation | 71 (10.5%) |
| quoted spans of four words or more | 99 |
| **found verbatim once normalised** | **94 (94.9%)** |
| found at no level of normalisation | 5 |

Then the five were read rather than counted, and **three of them were honest**:
`"from X to Y"` used as a schematic placeholder, and `"equal to or above"`
quoted twice *in order to say the policy does not use that wording*. A check
that fired on every unmatched quotation would have been **40% precise** —
rejecting three good answers to catch two bad ones.

What separates them is length, swept rather than guessed. Both honest spans are
four words; both fabrications are ten and seventeen. Every floor from five to
ten words rejects exactly the two fabrications and nothing else, so the rule is
eight words — the middle of that range, not the edge of a cliff. Quoting a
phrase in order to deny it is exempt at any length.

**This costs a correct answer, and that was written down before the run.**

| sixteen apply-the-rule questions | v0.12.10 | now |
|---|---:|---:|
| accuracy | 71.4% | **64.3%** |
| answers refused | 3 | **4** |
| false answers, 32 refusal questions | 0 | **0** |

64.3% was the predicted figure, to the case. The approver question is the only
one that changed: it now says *"I don't know"* rather than serving a right
answer on a fabricated citation. A gate that only ever fired on wrong answers
would not be a gate.

**And a metric turned out to be measuring the wrong thing.** Determinism on
that question read 100% before this release and 93.8% after — which looks like
a regression and is the opposite. The old run's second ask never reached the
model at all: the fabricated answer had been **cached**, so every later ask
replayed it word for word. Refusals are never cached, so now the second ask
really does call the model, and the model's second answer differed.

Caching a fabricated citation makes it look perfectly reproducible. What this
does *not* establish is how often the model invents one here — two generations
is not a rate.

Nothing in this release changes what a plain fact question does. Cached answers
are invalidated: the grounding policy moved g5 to g6.
