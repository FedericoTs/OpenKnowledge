**Rebuilding the index got faster, and the rebuild that changes nothing got
much faster.** The app rebuilds its index on every upload, every delete and
every access-rule change, in the request that is waiting for it. Measured on a
four-core machine, with no model involved:

| documents | rebuild after an upload | rebuild, nothing changed |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 | 3.17 s → **1.37 s** | 3.10 s → **0.19 s** |
| 300 | 26.4 s → **11.5 s** | 26.0 s → **0.66 s** |
| 500 | 71.1 s → **31.7 s** | 32.7 s → **1.47 s** |

Contradiction detection compares every pair of documents, and that was 98% of
an index. Two changes, neither of which alters a single contradiction found:

**A claim's weight is computed once.** Scoring a pair of claims needs the
weighted mass of each side and of what they share. The first two belong to a
claim, not to the pair, and were being re-added for every pair that claim
appeared in — 8.9 million additions over a few thousand distinct claims at 300
documents.

**A comparison over an unchanged corpus is not made twice.** An upload changes
the corpus, so nothing from before still applies and the work is done again. An
access-rule change does not change a single document, and used to pay the full
cost anyway: at 500 documents an administrator waited half a minute for an
answer that could not have moved.

Both were checked rather than argued. The contradictions found are
byte-identical to the previous release across every corpus in the repository,
and the labelled set still scores 100% precision and 100% recall.

**Indexing is still quadratic.** Going from 300 to 500 documents is 1.67 times
the corpus and 2.5 times the time. Both changes above are constants — a large
one on the path that runs most often, and no change at all to the shape of the
curve. A corpus in the thousands still costs minutes to index from scratch.
Before building a prefilter to skip document pairs, how much it would skip was
measured: 6.7% on a corpus of near-duplicates, 34.8% on a mixed one. That is a
smaller constant than the one already taken, so it was recorded and not built.

Retrieval is unaffected: queries stay in tens of milliseconds and memory in
hundreds of megabytes, both roughly linear in the size of the corpus.
